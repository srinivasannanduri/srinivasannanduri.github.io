Technical work asks us to care about correctness, performance and delivery. Leadership adds harder questions: who is affected by the work, and what kind of judgment we bring to it.

Three quotations I shared on X keep pulling me back to those questions. They're about purpose, perseverance and wonder. I don't want to turn them into slogans that explain every hard situation; I want to see what they mean in ordinary engineering work.

## Purpose: be a leader for good

*It is not enough to be a good leader; you must be a leader for good.* I don't know who first said it, but it names something that's easy to lose under delivery pressure.

"Do good" sounds too broad to guide an engineering decision. It gets concrete when you ask who benefits from a choice, who carries its cost, and who isn't in the room to say so.

Here's an ordinary example. A team can hit its date by shipping with an unclear recovery procedure and leaving operators to figure it out. The team's work gets smaller; the operators' work gets bigger, usually at 3 a.m. Once you look at the people inheriting that burden, the trade-off changes. It might justify a proper runbook, a simpler design, or at least an honest account of the limitation. Purpose mostly shows up in decisions like that: modest, specific, and important to whoever does the work after you.

## Perseverance: keep going, but know what you're keeping

*"I think a hero is an ordinary individual who finds the strength to persevere and endure in spite of overwhelming obstacles."* Christopher Reeve said that, and few people have known more about perseverance.

Difficulty is part of building anything worthwhile, and getting through it often takes patience, help, and a willingness to revise the plan. But persistence needs judgment. A team should be able to tell a valuable problem that needs a different approach from a commitment whose original assumptions no longer hold. Pushing on without reflection consumes people as well as time.

The useful question is: what are we trying to preserve? The outcome may still matter even when the current solution should change. Naming that difference makes it easier to ask for help, or to stop an unproductive path without feeling that you've given up on the goal.

## Wonder: keep the capacity to notice

*There are only two ways to live your life. One is as though nothing is a miracle. The other is as though everything is a miracle.* The line is usually attributed to Albert Einstein, though there's no reliable source for it.

Wonder has a real place in technical work. A beautifully simple explanation, an unexpected connection between two systems, a naive question from someone new to the domain: any of them can reveal something you'd stopped seeing. Make room for that attention. Read outside the immediate task. Ask why a familiar design actually works. Let a colleague explain how they see the problem.

Those habits renew curiosity without having to produce a deliverable. They also remind us that the people we work with bring far more than their current role or output.

## One human question

Before your next difficult decision, add one human question to the technical ones: *who will live with the consequences of this choice?*

It makes purpose practical. It gives perseverance a direction. And it leaves room for curiosity about a better way forward.

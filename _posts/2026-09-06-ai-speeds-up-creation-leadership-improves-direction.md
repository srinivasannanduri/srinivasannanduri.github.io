As AI accelerates software creation, I keep returning to one question: where should senior engineering leaders spend more of their attention?

When building gets cheaper, choosing what to build becomes the scarce skill. A working demo can now arrive before anyone has agreed on the problem, the users, or what success would look like. That doesn't make leadership less important. It moves it upstream, to three questions: is the problem relevant, what evidence would show it improved, and can teams make these calls without waiting for you?

## Relevance: does the problem matter?

"An AI assistant for operators" is a solution looking for a problem. "Operators spend too long finding the right runbook and working out whether it applies to the affected service" is a problem someone can investigate.

Once the friction is specific, the team can ask useful questions. Is the information missing, stale, or just hard to find? Would clear ownership metadata solve half of it? Which part genuinely needs AI? Those questions usually make the eventual product smaller and better. Sometimes they reveal that a simpler, unglamorous fix should come first, and that is a good outcome, not a failure of ambition.

The friction worth chasing is usually some mix of the three in the infographic: operator friction, uncertainty, and navigation time.

## Evidence: what change will we observe?

A demo proves that something can be produced. It says nothing about whether anyone's work got better.

For the operator example, the evidence is whether people reach the correct runbook, understand whether it applies, and take a sensible next step, measured against a baseline from before the change. Watch for the failure modes too: confident but wrong guidance, and cases where the assistant should have asked for more information instead of answering. A faster wrong answer is worse than a slow right one.

Any measure needs a definition and a baseline before the work starts, and it needs to stay anchored to the outcome rather than become the goal itself. That is Goodhart's warning, which I wrote about in [Improve the system. Protect the outcome.](/posts/improve-the-system-protect-the-outcome/) Done this way, evaluation becomes part of product discovery rather than a ceremony after the build.

## Distribution: can teams decide without you?

A senior leader who has to approve every decision becomes the bottleneck, and AI makes the queue grow faster. Direction should make everyday decisions easier for the team, not route them through one person.

What a team needs is intent, not instructions: the problem, the outcome that matters, the boundaries, and the evidence that would change the plan. Military planners call this commander's intent, and it works for the same reason in engineering. When conditions change, a team that understands *why* can adapt without asking. A short written brief carries that intent. Pair it with explicit ownership: who may change the approach, which risks need escalation, and when the team will revisit its assumptions. The same thinking applies to design reviews, which I wrote about in [Let risk choose the review](/posts/let-risk-choose-the-review/).

That frees leadership attention for the few decisions that genuinely need broader judgment.

## The test

The test of direction is what happens when you're not in the room. Can the team turn down an attractive feature because it doesn't reduce the chosen friction? Can it recognize evidence that the direction needs to change, and act on it?

For your next AI proposal, write the user's difficulty in one sentence, and name the observation that would convince you it has improved. Those two lines will shape a surprising amount of the work that follows.

*Vision without action is a daydream. Action without vision is a nightmare.*

The saying is usually described as a Japanese proverb, though nobody seems sure of its origin. Wherever it came from, it describes two failure modes every engineering organization knows. One team has a beautiful strategy deck and nothing shipped. Another ships constantly and can't say where it's going. The useful place is the corner where the two meet: directed progress.

## Turn direction into the next step

A direction is only useful if it changes what you do on Monday. "Make service onboarding easier" is a fine direction for a platform team, but it doesn't tell anyone what to do next.

Get closer to the user's task. Where do teams wait? Which decisions confuse them? Which steps need help from someone outside the team? Then pick one: observe one onboarding journey, and fix one recurring point of confusion. It's small enough to finish this week and clearly connected to the direction. I wrote about watching those journeys in [Hide the right complexity](/posts/hide-the-right-complexity/).

That beats a long improvement list that doesn't say which user difficulty each item addresses. It also gives the team an early chance to discover that its starting assumptions were wrong, while being wrong is still cheap.

## Momentum is built in silence

Momentum is built in silence long before it looks like success in public.

A lot of the most valuable work is hard to see while it's happening: learning a domain properly, simplifying an interface, earning the trust of a partner team. None of it makes a weekly announcement. All of it compounds. Sam Altman puts it bluntly in [What I Wish Someone Had Told Me](https://blog.samaltman.com/what-i-wish-someone-had-told-me): "compounding exponentials are magic".

Quiet work still needs a feedback loop, or it's indistinguishable from drift. Keep a short running record: the question you're investigating, what you changed, and what you learned. A small finished experiment is evidence of movement long before the bigger outcome appears. In the onboarding example, that might be the old point of confusion, the revised instructions, and what the next new team actually did with them.

## Simple principles, hard to practise

Altman's essay is one of those rare lists where almost every point lands. My own summary of it is: long-term thinking, outcomes over process, speed, focus, and great people. Two of its lines I come back to often: "outcomes are what count; don't let good process excuse bad results", and "fast iteration can make up for a lot; it's usually ok to be wrong if you iterate quickly". And, simply: "working with great people is one of the best parts of life."

None of these is hard to understand. They're hard to practise consistently, on an ordinary Tuesday, when the urgent and the important pull in different directions.

## Revisit direction without losing focus

A direction should be stable enough to guide choices, and open enough to change when the evidence does. Decide in advance what would make you reconsider it: a new constraint, a different user need, or repeated failure to move the outcome you chose.

Focus also means saying no to attractive work that doesn't serve the current direction, and explaining why, so the team can make the same call next time without you. That's the same discipline I described in [AI speeds up creation. Leadership improves direction.](/posts/ai-speeds-up-creation-leadership-improves-direction/)

Pick one ambition you care about. Write down the next action you can actually finish, and the observation that will tell you what to do after that. Direction becomes useful when it shapes the work in front of you.

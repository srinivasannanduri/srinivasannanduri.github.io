Career growth in engineering, in one line each:

*I can solve it. I can own it. I can lead it. I can align others around it. I can set direction. I can shape strategy.*

Your code may stop scaling. Your judgment and influence shouldn't.

## The question changes before the title does

The question I hear most often from engineers I mentor is: "What should I do to grow to the next level?" The shift I've consistently seen matter is in the questions they ask.

Early in your career, you ask: *how do I solve this?* As you grow, you start asking:

- *Should* we solve this?
- Why now?
- What happens if we don't?
- Is there a simpler way?

Technical depth helps you solve problems. Engineering judgment helps you choose which problems are worth solving. People usually start asking the second set of questions well before anyone changes their title, and that's often what earns the change.

## Each step moves the uncertainty upstream

Take one request and follow it up the steps: *build a shared configuration service.*

- **Solve:** implement it well. The uncertainty is technical.
- **Own:** understand who consumes it, who operates it, and how it behaves when it fails. You're now responsible for the outcome, not just the code.
- **Lead:** plan and sequence the work, make the trade-offs, unblock the people building it with you.
- **Align:** get the consuming teams to agree on the contract. The hard part is usually here, not in storage or distribution. Real alignment resolves conflicting assumptions and responsibilities; a meeting where everyone politely agrees often leaves them intact.
- **Direct:** ask whether a new service is justified at all. Perhaps existing capabilities already cover the important needs, and the right move is to say no to another system, a point I made in [Scale tests every assumption](/posts/scale-tests-every-assumption/).
- **Shape strategy:** look across many requests like this one and decide what the organization should standardize on, so the next ten teams don't each face the same question.

At every step the code still matters. What changes is where the hardest uncertainty sits: first in *how*, then in *who*, then in *whether*.

## Depth is the foundation, not the rung you leave behind

It's tempting to read the ladder as "stop coding and start talking". That's backwards. The most credible "should we build this?" comes from someone who could build it, and knows what it would really cost. Judgment without depth is just opinion.

Tanya Reilly's *The Staff Engineer's Path* describes the senior technical role as a mix of big-picture thinking, project execution, and leveling up the people around you. All three depend on staying close enough to the technology to be right about it.

## It isn't a universal ladder

The sequence is a way to think about growing reach, not a promise that every organization uses the same titles, or that everyone should want the same role. A deep specialist can shape strategy through insight into one critical area. Someone else can grow by making a team much better at decisions within its current scope. The useful question is which responsibility you're learning to carry well.

## Practise on a real decision

You don't need a promotion to start. Pick a piece of work already within reach and write down:

1. the outcome it's meant to achieve;
2. the alternatives, including doing nothing for now, and what that would cost;
3. who will operate the result and who will depend on it.

Bring those people in early enough that they can still change the decision. Then explain the choice simply enough that another engineer could repeat why it was made. Writing it down is half the work, which I wrote about in [Make your judgment reusable](/posts/make-your-judgment-reusable/).

Ask for feedback on your judgment, not just your output. Did you find the real constraint? Make the uncertainty visible? Involve the right owners? Help the group choose without hiding the trade-offs?

For your next project, add one question before "how": should we solve this, why now, what happens if we wait, or is there a simpler way? Follow the answer through to a decision. That is what growth looks like from the inside.
